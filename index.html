The concept

What is dyslexia — and what it isn't

The definition

Dyslexia is a specific learning difficulty that affects accurate and fluent reading, spelling and written expression. It is neurological in origin and occurs across the full range of intellectual abilities.

It is a lifelong condition — not a sign of low intelligence.

✗ Myth
Dyslexia is just about reading slowly or getting letters the wrong way round.
✓ Fact
Dyslexia affects processing speed, working memory, phonological awareness and organisation — not just reading. Many adults are highly able in verbal reasoning and creativity.

Source: British Dyslexia Association / Rose Report 2009

The concept

How adults present differently

Adult dyslexia is often missed because learners have spent decades developing coping strategies. By the time they reach your session, the dyslexia may be invisible — but the impact isn't.

🗣️
AvoidanceTalking a lot to avoid being asked to read aloud, or volunteering for physical tasks instead of written ones.
⏱️
Slow completionTaking significantly longer with written tasks, or leaving them unfinished and offering other reasons.
😤
Frustration or disengagementBehaviour that looks like attitude is often shame or anxiety about being exposed.
🧠
Strong verbal abilityArticulate in discussion but written work looks inconsistent. A gap between spoken and written performance is a key indicator.
📋
Copying errorsMiscopying from boards or handouts — not because of poor eyesight but because of working memory load.
Worked example

Marcus — what you might see

Learner profile

Marcus is 28 and on a Restart programme. He's confident in group discussions, contributes good ideas, and has strong practical skills from his previous job in logistics.

When you give out a written activity, Marcus goes quiet. He takes his sheet but doesn't start. After 10 minutes he says he's "not bothered" and puts it face down.

What's likely happening

Marcus can't process the written task quickly enough to keep up with the group. The fear of being seen to struggle — after years of hiding it — is greater than the task itself. "Not bothered" is protective.

What a good trainer does

Doesn't push. Quietly offers to talk through the activity verbally first, or pair Marcus with someone. Doesn't draw attention. Notes the pattern for a private conversation later.

What you can do

Practical strategies for your sessions

These don't require any specialist training — they are reasonable adjustments that benefit all learners, not just those with dyslexia.

🔤
Font and layoutUse sans-serif fonts (Arial, Calibri) at minimum 14pt. Left-align text. Never justify. Use generous line spacing.
🎨
Coloured backgroundsOffer cream or pastel alternatives to white paper. White with black text creates high contrast glare for some dyslexic learners.
🔊
Give instructions verbally firstSay it, then write it. Don't rely on learners reading from the board or handout as their primary source.
⏰
Extended time without fussBuild extra processing time into activities for everyone — you don't need to single anyone out.
🎤
Alternative ways to respondVerbal responses, recording answers on a phone, drawing, or completing with a partner all count. Reduce the barrier, not the expectation.
🧩
Chunk and sequenceBreak activities into small steps. Give one instruction at a time. Don't overload working memory.

Key reminder

You don't need to diagnose anyone. Your role is to notice patterns, reduce barriers, and refer to your SENCO or pastoral lead when a learner may benefit from a formal assessment.
